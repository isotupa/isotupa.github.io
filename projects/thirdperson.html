ThirdPerson: flying a drone by standing in front of it

No controller, no phone. The drone watches you from a few metres away, like a third-person camera in a game, and you direct it with your hands.

Role
Sole author. Trabajo Fin de Grado, carried out with the CVAR robotics lab.
Stack
Python, MediaPipe, TensorFlow Lite, OpenCV, djitellopy, Docker
Hardware
DJI Tello, with a webcam simulation mode that runs the whole pipeline without one
When
2023–2024

The problem with gesture control at a distance

Point a hand detector at a full video frame with a person three metres away and it misses the hand, or it finds something that is not a hand. Hands are small, they are far from the camera, and there is a lot of frame for the detector to be wrong in. Every approach that treats the whole image as the search space runs into this.

ThirdPerson gets around it by leaning on the body first. The body is large, it is easy to find, and once you have it you know where the hands are.

camera frame ──► pose landmarker ──► crop around right wrist ──► hand landmarker

                       │                                              │

                                              │
                                              ▼

The pose landmarker runs on every frame and locates the person and their limbs. From that, a small window around the right wrist is cropped and the hand landmarker only ever sees the crop. Far less to search, far more reliable at range. The 21 hand landmarks are then translated to the wrist and scaled, and fed to a small TFLite classifier trained on recordings I made of each gesture myself.

The part that stops the drone twitching

Per-frame classification is noisy, and a drone that acts on every frame is a drone that jerks every time the classifier flickers. Raw predictions go through a short buffer that only commits to a gesture once it has been the majority vote over the last several frames. It costs a fraction of a second of latency and it is the difference between a demo and something you would stand in front of.

Two ways to fly

In manual mode each gesture maps to a movement command the drone holds until you give it another one. In follow mode the drone locks onto your body and keeps you centred at a set distance on its own — a separate controller reads the shoulder and nose landmarks to estimate how far away and how off-centre you are and turns that into smooth velocity commands. Gestures still work in follow mode, but instead of driving the drone they adjust how far away it should sit and where it should frame you.

What made it testable

The drone sits behind an abstract DroneController interface, and the real Tello and a webcam simulator are interchangeable implementations of it. Everything upstream — pose, hands, gestures, the GUI — has no idea which one it is talking to. Out of the box the project starts in simulation mode against your laptop camera, drawing what it would do without touching hardware.

This is not just a convenience. Iterating on a gesture classifier is a loop you want to run hundreds of times, and a loop that requires charging a battery, finding a room and accepting that a mistake means a drone hitting a wall is a loop you run five times a day.

What I would fix

A few gestures are in the code and disabled: a scripted half-circle, a barrel roll, and switching to a different person to follow. They worked on and off, and I ran out of runway before the deadline to make them reliable. Shipping them half-working would have been worse than shipping them off.

The tuning is for indoor use at roughly two to four metres and it is happiest against a plain background. This is research code written under a deadline, not a product — and flying a real drone with it means the drone actually moves, so it wants space and a hand near the keyboard. There are keyboard fallbacks for every command precisely because a gesture system that only accepts gestures is a system with no way out.
